For a product that spans multiple industries, the **primary industry should usually be determined by the product’s core economic and customer value proposition**, not simply by every technology or capability it uses.

The cleanest way to determine it is to ask: **“What business is this product fundamentally in?”**

A strong classification framework is:

| Factor | Question | Typical weight |
|---|---|---:|
| **Primary customer problem** | What main problem is the customer paying to solve? | **25%** |
| **Core product/service delivered** | What does the product fundamentally provide? | **20%** |
| **Primary revenue source** | What activity generates most of the revenue? | **20%** |
| **Primary customer perception** | What type of company/product would customers say this is? | **15%** |
| **Competitive set** | Which companies/products does it primarily compete against? | **10%** |
| **Operating model** | What business activities dominate operations? | **5%** |
| **Regulatory/industry environment** | Which industry's rules most materially govern the product? | **5%** |

The highest-scoring industry becomes the **primary industry**, while other meaningful categories become **secondary industries** or **cross-industry classifications**.

For example, imagine an AI-powered personal finance app that analyzes spending, recommends budgets, and uses a generative AI assistant.

Its industries might include:

- Artificial Intelligence
- Software/SaaS
- Fintech
- Personal Finance
- Data & Analytics

But its **primary industry would normally be Fintech/Personal Finance**, because the main customer problem is managing money, its competitors are financial apps, and the economic value delivered is financial management. AI is an enabling technology rather than the fundamental business.

A similar distinction matters with e-commerce. Suppose a company operates an AI-powered fashion marketplace.

It could be classified as:

**Primary:** Fashion/E-commerce  
**Secondary:** Online Marketplace  
**Supporting/enabling:** Artificial Intelligence, Recommendation Technology, Payments

The fact that AI is technically central does not automatically make it an AI-industry product.

### A useful hierarchy

For a sophisticated multi-industry company, I would use **four classification layers** rather than forcing everything into one label:

**1. Primary Industry**  
The economic/business domain the product primarily serves.

**2. Secondary Industries**  
Other industries in which the product meaningfully participates.

**3. Business Model**  
How the company actually makes money.

Examples: SaaS, marketplace, subscription, advertising, transaction fees, e-commerce, licensing.

**4. Technology / Capability Categories**  
Technologies enabling the product.

Examples: AI, cloud computing, cybersecurity, blockchain, geospatial technology.

This prevents a common classification mistake. Consider:

> **AI-powered SaaS platform that helps hospitals optimize staffing**

A poor classification might be:

> AI industry

A better classification is:

**Primary industry:** Healthcare / HealthTech  
**Secondary industry:** HR/workforce technology  
**Business model:** B2B SaaS  
**Technology:** Artificial intelligence, analytics, optimization

That describes the actual business much more accurately.

### When revenue should dominate the classification

Revenue is especially useful when the product genuinely serves several equally important purposes.

Suppose a platform contains:

- online courses,
- a professional community,
- job listings,
- recruiting services,
- paid subscriptions,
- advertising.

If 65% of its economic activity comes from recruiting and employer fees, it would likely be primarily **Recruiting/HR**, even though consumers may spend substantial time consuming educational content.

Conversely, if 70% of revenue comes from course subscriptions, **Education/EdTech** is likely the primary industry.

However, revenue should **not be used mechanically**. Early-stage products often have immature revenue streams. In that case, intended customer value and business model should carry more weight.

### Regulatory dominance can override the normal classification

There are certain products where the industry carrying the greatest regulatory significance should strongly influence classification.

For example:

> AI system that evaluates borrowers and makes lending decisions.

Even though it is an AI software product, it belongs primarily in **financial services/lending/fintech**, because financial regulations govern the core activity.

Likewise:

> Telemedicine platform using AI diagnosis support.

Primary: **Healthcare / Digital Health**  
Secondary: AI / SaaS

The regulated activity matters more than the enabling technology.

### Competitive-set test

One of the most useful practical tests is:

> **If the product disappeared tomorrow, which companies would customers consider as alternatives?**

That often reveals the primary industry immediately.

For example, if an AI writing product competes mainly with Microsoft Word, Google Docs, Grammarly, and Notion, it is probably primarily **productivity/software**, not AI.

If an AI legal research platform competes primarily with Westlaw and LexisNexis, it belongs primarily in **LegalTech**, even though AI may be its central technology.

### The "remove the technology" test

Another good test is:

> **If you removed the enabling technology, what underlying service would still be provided?**

Examples:

- AI investment assistant → **Investment/Fintech**
- AI tutoring app → **Education**
- AI fitness coach → **Fitness/Wellness**
- AI advertising optimizer → **Advertising/MarTech**
- AI cybersecurity analyst → **Cybersecurity**
- AI travel planner → **Travel**
- AI customer-support platform → **Customer Service Software**

AI describes **how** the product works. The industry often describes **what economic problem it solves**.

### A practical decision rule

For your kind of multi-domain app portfolio, I would define the primary industry formally as:

> **The industry representing the greatest combination of primary customer value, economic activity, competitive positioning, and regulatory/business context of the product.**

Then classify each product like this:

**Primary Industry → Secondary Industry(s) → Business Model → Product Category → Enabling Technologies**

For example:

> **AI-powered online marketplace connecting homeowners with contractors**

could become:

**Primary Industry:** Home Services  
**Secondary Industries:** Construction Technology, Online Marketplaces  
**Business Model:** Two-sided marketplace / transaction fees  
**Product Category:** Contractor discovery and booking  
**Technology:** AI matching, payments, reputation systems

And:

> **Mobile game with social networking, virtual items, advertisements, and an AI-generated content system**

could be:

**Primary Industry:** Gaming  
**Secondary Industries:** Social Media, Digital Entertainment  
**Business Model:** Freemium + in-app purchases + advertising  
**Product Category:** Mobile social game  
**Technology:** Generative AI, recommendation systems

This approach is particularly useful for a company with many products because it gives you **one stable primary category for reporting and portfolio management without losing the product's multi-industry nature**.

# Evaluating Industry Growth and Realistic Business Opportunity Across All 20 Industries

Yes. Both can be evaluated systematically, using multiple complementary metrics. In fact, I recommend treating them as two independent assessments because a rapidly growing industry is not necessarily an attractive industry for a new online-only company.

The ideal system would produce three results for every industry:

1. Industry Growth Score (0–100): Is the industry expanding, stable, or contracting? At what rate, and how sustainably?
2. Realistic Opportunity Score (0–100): Can a new online-only company realistically enter this industry, acquire customers, generate revenue, and become profitable?
3. Combined Investment Attractiveness Score (0–100): How appealing is this industry relative to the other 19, considering growth, economics, competition, risks, and execution feasibility?

For a company planning to build multiple digital products, these assessments should operate at both the overall industry level and the narrower product-market level.

## 1. Industry Growth Evaluation

An industry can grow in revenue while customer demand is stagnating, or grow in customer adoption while profitability deteriorates. Therefore, relying on one measurement, such as TAM growth, is insufficient.

I recommend evaluating eight independent growth dimensions.

| Growth dimension                | Weight | What to measure                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Market revenue growth           | 25%    | Historical and projected annual revenue growth; CAGR        |
| Customer demand growth          | 20%    | Paid adoption, customer counts, purchasing demand           |
| Digital adoption growth         | 15%    | Migration toward online delivery and digital transactions   |
| Forward growth outlook          | 15%    | Forecast CAGR, emerging demand, structural drivers          |
| Investment & business formation | 10%    | Capital investment, new businesses, product launches        |
| Revenue quality                 | 5%     | Real versus inflation-driven growth; recurring revenue      |
| Growth consistency              | 5%     | Stability across multiple years and economic cycles         |
| Structural resilience           | 5%     | Resistance to displacement, regulation, and economic shocks |
| Total                           | 100%   |                                                             |

These are recommended model weights, not externally validated industry standards.

### Key mathematical measurements

Year-over-year growth

\\[ g_t=\frac{M_t-M\_{t-1}}{M\_{t-1}}\times100 \\]

Compound annual growth rate (CAGR)

\\[ CAGR=\left(\frac{M\_{\text{end}}}{M\_{\text{start}}}\right)^{1/n}-1 \\]

Growth acceleration

\\[ a_t=g_t-g\_{t-1} \\]

These separate an industry that is expanding from one that is merely expanding more slowly.

### Industry growth classification

The following thresholds are proposed screening rules, not universal economic definitions.

| Annual real revenue growth | Classification          | Interpretation                  |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Above +20%                 | Exceptional expansion   | Very rapid growth               |
| +10% to +20%               | High growth             | Strong expansion                |
| +5% to +10%                | Moderate growth         | Healthy expansion               |
| Above 0% to +5%            | Slow growth             | Expanding gradually             |
| 0%                         | Stagnant                | No real growth                  |
| −5% to below 0%            | Mild contraction        | Industry shrinking              |
| Below −5%                  | Significant contraction | Sustained decline if persistent |

Because these are inflation-adjusted growth rates, a company cannot mistake higher prices for greater underlying economic activity.

## 2. Realistic Business Opportunity Evaluation

Industry growth alone does not determine whether a new entrant can succeed.

A $500 billion mature market with dominant incumbents and expensive customer acquisition could be less attractive than a $5 billion niche market with an underserved customer base, strong demand, and high margins.

For an online-only company, I recommend the following 10-dimensional model.

| Opportunity dimension             | Weight | What is evaluated                                                        |
| --------------------------------- | ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Addressable revenue opportunity   | 15%    | Product-specific SAM and attainable SOM                                  |
| Unmet demand                      | 15%    | Customer pain, underserved segments, willingness to pay                  |
| Competitive accessibility         | 15%    | Incumbent advantages, market concentration, differentiation              |
| Profit potential                  | 15%    | Gross margins, contribution margins, sustainable net profitability       |
| Customer acquisition efficiency   | 10%    | CAC, organic reach, distribution opportunities                           |
| Retention & monetization          | 10%    | Churn, LTV, purchase frequency, revenue retention                        |
| Technical & operating feasibility | 8%     | Automation suitability, infrastructure, reliability, delivery complexity |
| Regulatory & security feasibility | 5%     | Compliance requirements and manageable risks                             |
| Speed to revenue                  | 4%     | Sales cycle, launch time, time until initial revenue                     |
| Defensibility & scalability       | 3%     | Switching costs, network effects, moats, operating leverage              |
| Total                             | 100%   |                                                                          |

A score should incorporate uncertainty and evidence quality rather than reward speculative assumptions.

For example, an AI application with a large SAM but no proof that customers will pay should not receive a high opportunity score merely because AI spending is expanding.

## 7. How I would implement this as a repeatable business intelligence system

Define 20 industries and their subindustries

Collect historical and projected market data

Normalize metrics and verify sources

Calculate Industry Growth Score

Research customer demand and competitive accessibility

Model SAM, SOM, acquisition costs and profitability

Calculate Realistic Opportunity Score

Apply feasibility, risk and evidence gates

Rank industries and identify attractive product niches

Reassess when important market signals change

Continuous monitoring and re-evaluation

For automation, I recommend:

- Monthly: Update high-frequency indicators such as demand, competitor activity, app adoption and pricing.
- Quarterly: Recalculate industry growth, opportunity scores, market forecasts and prioritization.
- Annually: Reassess market definitions, scoring weights, long-term trends and model performance.
- Event-driven: Trigger targeted reassessment when major regulations, new competitors, technology breakthroughs or significant economic changes occur.

Each score should carry source references, timestamps, confidence levels and a record of how it was calculated.

### Final recommendation

Rather than asking only whether an industry is growing, your company should be able to answer four questions for all 20 industries:

1. Is the industry growing? By how much, and is growth accelerating or decelerating?
2. Is the digitally serviceable portion growing? An industry's offline business could stagnate while its online sector expands rapidly.
3. Are attractive opportunities available to new entrants? This depends on unmet demand, competition, achievable revenue, costs, and risks.
4. Which specific product niches offer the highest risk-adjusted probability of commercial success?

The fourth question is ultimately the most valuable for deciding which online-only applications to build.

A continuously updated, evidence-backed system using these four evaluations would be substantially more reliable than ranking industries solely by TAM, SAM, market capitalization, or CAGR.

## 6. Confidence and what remains to be validated

This screening is useful for selecting where to investigate first, but it is not yet an investment-grade industry ranking.

Growth evidence is strongest for software, AI infrastructure, cybersecurity, education, digital health, advertising, and gaming. Opportunity is less directly observable because actual new-entrant profitability depends on product-specific data that industry forecasts do not provide.

Before treating any category as a confirmed high-opportunity market, I would require evidence of paying demand, achievable customer acquisition, realistic SAM and SOM, competitive differentiation, attainable gross and net margins, and regulatory feasibility.

The industry scores should also be revisited when narrower segments are selected. A niche within one of the 12 lower-ranked categories could easily outperform a generic offering in one of the eight high-ranked categories.

Final result: Eight categories provisionally qualify. My strongest initial research priorities would be Software/SaaS/AI (#1), Cloud/Cybersecurity/Data/Developer Technology (#14), and Marketing/Advertising/Creator Economy (#7). Education (#6), Legal/Compliance (#16), and AI-enabled Professional Services (#9) are particularly worth investigating for narrowly targeted, relatively lean online products.

The eight industries classified as Lower Growth / Lower Opportunity were placed there because of a combination of relatively moderate industry growth, competitive saturation, operational complexity, customer acquisition costs, and challenges achieving sustainable profitability for a new online-only business.

Importantly, “lower growth” does not mean declining, and “lower opportunity” does not mean unprofitable. Both are relative to the higher-growth, more accessible categories in the previous assessment.

### What actually drove the lower-opportunity assessments?

Across these eight industries, the recurring barriers were:

1. Competition: Established businesses dominate significant portions of demand and distribution.
2. Customer acquisition: Attracting paying users may require substantial spending.
3. Profit margins: Fulfillment, content production, support, platform fees, and other operating costs can reduce profitability.
4. Differentiation: Some products are easy for competitors to imitate.
5. Execution complexity: Certain markets require external suppliers, operational partners, specialized expertise, or regulatory compliance.
6. Revenue predictability: Some business models rely heavily on individual hits, seasonal demand, transactions, or economic conditions rather than recurring subscriptions.

These are relevant risk factors, not proof that every business within those industries faces the same obstacles.

### My revised interpretation

I would distinguish the eight industries as follows:

| Industry                 | More precise interpretation                                                         |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| E-commerce               | Growing; opportunity highly dependent on margins, differentiation, and product type |
| Media & Publishing       | Mixed growth; difficult broad-market monetization, attractive specialized niches    |
| Gaming                   | Growing moderately; potentially high upside but highly variable commercial outcomes |
| Employment & HR          | Cyclical core market; attractive specialized software opportunities                 |
| Real Estate & PropTech   | Cyclical industry; opportunity concentrated in operational efficiency tools         |
| Travel & Hospitality     | Growing digital activity; difficult consumer-platform competition                   |
| Logistics & Supply Chain | Mixed growth; specialized B2B software can be attractive                            |
| Consumer Lifestyle       | Fragmented; opportunity depends heavily on niche, retention, and business model     |

The most important conclusion: I would not exclude any of these eight industries from an automated product-discovery system.

Instead, I would apply the growth/opportunity quadrant classification at the subindustry and product-niche levels, not use the parent industry's score as an automatic disqualification.

For example, broad gaming may score below the high-growth threshold, while a specific emerging gaming segment could offer stronger growth and attractive economics. Similarly, e-commerce infrastructure software could be a considerably better opportunity than launching another general-purpose online retail store.

This would allow a product-discovery system to identify commercially promising opportunities in all 20 industries without assuming that a broad industry ranking accurately represents every business within it.
